Likewise, on Spirits

Similar to this is what is said of Edric Wilde, that is 'The Woodsman', so called from the agility of his body and the charm of his words and works, a man of great honesty, and the lord of Ledbury of the north; who on returning late from the hunt through a lonely spot at midnight, unsure of the way, went astray, accompanied by only a boy, and came to a large house on the edge of a forest, such as drinking houses that Englishmen used to have, one to a diocese, called a guildhouse in English. And when he was near he saw a light in it, on looking in he saw many remarkable women dancing in a great ring.

Now they were most beautiful in appearance, and in lovely garments so elegantly dressed in linen alone, bigger and taller than ours are.

Yet the aforementioned soldier took note of one among the others, surpassing the rest in form and appearance, more desirable than all the darlings of kings.

They circled with a polished motion and pleasant carriage, and with restrained voice, a tiny sound of awe-inspiring harmony was heard, but not one of their words was intelligible.

On seeing this, the soldier took a wound to the heart, and barely restrained his passions, marked with Cupid's bow, and was emboldened by a heat of beautiful pestilence and splendid crises.

AJC He had heard of people's mistakes, and the nocturnal battalions of spirits and their deadly visions, Dictinna, and the assembly of Dryads and spectral squadrons, and he'd been told of the vengeance of offended deities - how they inflict unexpected punishments in their unexpected visitations, how they keep themselves safe, and dwell apart in secret and unknown, how they have spectators who try to catch their counsels to disclose them, who probe them to reveal them, how much they shut themselves up in anxiety lest, seen, they become objects of contempt. He'd heard of vengeance and examples of the avenged; but because he was justly painted blind by Cupid, and heedless of everything, he thinks not on the apparition, sees not the avenger, and, because he has no light, thoughtlessly gives offense.

AJC He circles the house, and, discovering an entrance, rushes in, seizes the one who captivated him and at once pulls her away from the others, and holding her fast in a lengthy, vigorous struggle he was rescued by his boy and his own mighty efforts, and not entirely uninjured, but, as much could women's nails and teeth do, struck with feet and flutes; he took her with him, and enjoyed her for a vow for three days and nights, but could not wrench a word out of her, yet she allowed, with a placid consent, venery for his pleasure.

AJC Indeed, on the fourth day she spoke these words to him: "Be well, my sweetest, and you will be well, and you'll rejoice in the prosperous standing of person and things while you reproach to me the sisters from whom, or the place or grove from where I was carried off, or anything about that. Indeed from this day on you'll fall from fortune, and from seizing me you'll fail in frequent defeat, and with your importunity you'll meet your final day the sooner.
