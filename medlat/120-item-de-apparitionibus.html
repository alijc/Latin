Similar to this is what is said of Edric Wilde, that is 'The Woodsman', so called from the agility of his body and the charm of his words and works, a man of great honesty, and the lord of Ledbury of the north; who on returning late from the hunt through a lonely spot at midnight, unsure of the way, went astray, accompanied by only a boy, and came to a large house on the edge of a forest, such as drinking houses that Englishmen used to have, one to a diocese, called a guildhouse in English. And when he was near he saw a light in it, on looking in he saw many remarkable women dancing in a great ring.

Now they were most beautiful in appearance, and in lovely garments so elegantly dressed in linen alone, bigger and taller than ours are.

Yet the aforementioned soldier took note of one among the others, surpassing the rest in form and appearance, more desirable than all the darlings of kings.

They circled with a polished motion and pleasant carriage, and with restrained voice, a tiny sound of awe-inspiring harmony was heard, but not one of their words was intelligible.

On seeing this, the soldier took a wound to the heart, and barely restrained his passions, marked with Cupid's bow, and was emboldened by a heat of beautiful pestilence and splendid crises.

He had heard of the pagans' errors, and the nocturnal battalions of spirits and the deadly visions of them, Dictinna, and the assembly of Dryads and spectral squadrons, and he'd been told of the vengeance of offended deities - how they inflict unexpected punishments on unexpected visitors, how they keep themselves safe, and dwell apart in secret and unknown, how they hate those who try to discover their plans to disclose them, who search them out to reveal them, how carefully they shut themselves up lest, seen, they become objects of contempt. He'd heard of examples of vengeance and punishment; but because Cupid is rightly depicted as blind, and heedless of everything, he thinks it not a phantom, perceives it not an avenger, and, because he sees not the light, thoughtlessly gives offense.

He circles the house, and, discovering an entrance, rushes in, seizes the one who captivated him and is at once seized by the others, and held for some time in a ferocious struggle, he's rescued by his boy and his own mighty efforts, and not entirely unharmed, but, wounded in the feet and calfs as much women's nails and teeth could do; he took her with him and enjoyed her as he wished for three days and nights, but could not wrench a word out of her, yet she allowed, with a placid consent, venery for his pleasure.

Indeed, on the fourth day she spoke these words to him: "Be well, my sweetest, and you will be well, and you'll rejoice in the prosperous standing of person and possessions until you reproach me or the sisters from whom, or the place or grove from whence I was carried off, or anything about it. Indeed from thaat day on you'll fall from fortune, and after I've been taken away you'll fail in frequent defeat, and with your importunity you'll meet your final day the sooner.

He promised, with whatever pledge he could give, that he would be ever steadfast and faithful in his love.

William the Bastard, then the new king of England, ruled in those days. On hearing of this marvel, eager to examine and to plainly understand what the truth might be, he summoned them both to come to London at once, and many witnesses came with them, bringing the dispositions of many who could not be present, and there was great evidence of her supernatural character, a type of woman never yet seen or heard of, and with the stupefaction of all they were sent back to their own home. It later came to pass, after the passage of many years, that Edric was returning from hunting around the third hour of the night when he looked for her but couldn't find her, and called her and ordered her to be called, and then seeing her slowly approaching, angrily said: "Surely, you weren't detained this long by your sisters?" and tossed other invectives into thin air, for she'd vanished on hearing 'sisters'.

And so the young man regretted his angry outbursts, so irregular and damming, and made for the spot where he'd carried her off, but with no weeping, with no wailing could he regain her.
