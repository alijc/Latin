The Abbot Samson

While the abbacy was vacant, the prior above all strove to maintain peace in the manstery and to preserve the honor of the church by accepting guests, to disturb no-one willingly, to provoke no-one to anger, to keep everyone and everything in peace. Yet he ignored some matters that should've been corrected about our officials, and especially about the sacristan, as he didn't care what he did about the sacristy, he had not paid any debt or built anything and during the time in which the abbacy was vacant - but the offerings and revenues were foolishly wasted.

And because of this, the prior, who served as head of the monastery, seemed blameworthy to many of them and was called remiss.

And our brothers reminded each other of this when it came to making the choice of an abbot.

Our cellarer accepted all guests, whatever their condition might be, at the monastery's expense.

Willelm, the sacristan, gave and paid out on his own inititiative; a bountiful man, he gave what should be given and what should not, blinding the eyes of all with his gifts.

Samson, the under-sacristan, the overseer of the workers, left nothing broken, nothing torn up, nothing split, nothing unrepaired if he could help it; and from this he endeared himself to the monastery and particularly to the cloistered monks in gratitude.

During those days our choir was erected under Samson's management, and he also arranged the paintings and composed their inscriptions in elegiac verse.

He provided a great supply of stones and sand for the construction of a large church tower.

And when he was asked where he got the coin to do this, he replied that some burgers had quietly given him money for erecting and finishing the tower.

But some of our brothers said that our monk Warin, the shrine's custodian, and Samson the under-sacristan had banded together to furtively steal a portion of the shrine's offerings, in order to use it to pay for the church's necessities and expressly for building the tower. They came to this conclusion since they saw that the offerings were being spent for extraordinary uses by others, who, I may rightly say, were stealing them.

And in order to remove any suspicion of their successful theft, the aforesaid duo of men built a chest, hollow with a hole in the middle of the top and bolted with an iron bar. They had it placed in the great church near the doorway in the public passage outside the sanctuary, so people could put their alms there for building the tower.

Indeed, the sacristan Willelm mistrusted his comrade Samson, and there were many others who took Willelm's side, both Christians and Jews.

The Jews, I say, called the sacristan father and patron, and were glad of his protection and were free to come and go, and went everywhere through the monastery, wandering past the altar and around the biers while the solemnities of mass were being celebrated. And their money was stored in our treasury under the protection of the sacristan and, what is more absurd, their wives and little ones were lodged in our quarters in times of war.

And so, his foes or adversaries devised a plan by which they might attack Samson, and met with Robert of Cokefeld and the other warden of the abbey and induced them to, for the king's sake, keep anyone from doing any work or building for however long the abbacy was vacant; but rather to collect and preserve the money from the offerings for paying their debts.

And thus was Samson mocked and his strength withdrew from him; nor could anything else that he wished be done.
