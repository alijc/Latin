The Abbot Samson

While the abbacy was vacant, the prior above all strove to maintain peace in the manstery and to preserve the honor of the church by accepting guests, to disturb no-one willingly, to provoke no-one to anger, to keep everyone and everything in peace. Yet he ignored some matters that should've been corrected about our officials, and especially about the sacristan, as he didn't care what he did about the sacristy, he had not paid any debt or built anything and during the time in which the abbacy was vacant - but the offerings and revenues were foolishly wasted.

And because of this, the prior, who served as head of the monastery, seemed blameworthy to many of them and was called remiss.

And our brothers reminded each other of this when it came to making the choice of an abbot.

Our cellarer accepted all guests, whatever their condition might be, at the monastery's expense.

Willelm, the sacristan, gave and paid out on his own inititiative; a bountiful man, he gave what should be given and what should not, blinding the eyes of all with his gifts.

Samson, the under-sacristan, the overseer of the workers, left nothing broken, nothing torn up, nothing split, nothing unrepaired if he could help it; and from this he endeared himself to the monastery and particularly to the cloistered monks in gratitude.

During those days our choir was erected under Samson's management, and he also arranged the paintings and composed their inscriptions in elegiac verse.

He provided a great supply of stones and sand for the construction of a large church tower.

And when he was asked where he got the coin to do this, he replied that some burgers had quietly given him money for erecting and finishing the tower.

But some of our brothers said that our monk Warin, the shrine's custodian, and Samson the under-sacristan had banded together to furtively steal a portion of the shrine's offerings, in order to use it to pay for the church's necessities and expressly for building the tower. They came to this conclusion since they saw that the offerings were being spent for extraordinary uses by others, who, I may rightly say, were stealing them.

And in order to remove any suspicion of their successful theft, the aforesaid duo of men built a chest, hollow with a hole in the middle of the top and bolted with an iron bar. They had it placed in the great church near the doorway in the public passage outside the sanctuary, so people could put their alms there for building the tower.

Indeed, the sacristan Willelm mistrusted his comrade Samson, and there were many others who took Willelm's side, both Christians and Jews.

The Jews, I say, called the sacristan father and patron, and were glad of his protection and were free to come and go, and went everywhere through the monastery, wandering past the altar and around the biers while the solemnities of mass were being celebrated. And their money was stored in our treasury under the protection of the sacristan and, what is more absurd, their wives and little ones were lodged in our quarters in times of war.

And so, his foes or adversaries devised a plan by which they might attack Samson, and met with Robert of Cokefeld and the other warden of the abbey and induced them to, for the king's sake, keep anyone from doing any work or building for however long the abbacy was vacant; but rather to collect and preserve the money from the offerings for paying their debts.

And thus was Samson mocked and his strength withdrew from him; nor could anything else that he wished be done.

Indeed, his adversaries could postpone the matter but not stop it completely; for, when his strength was recovered and the two columns toppled, that is, when both abbey wardens, who supported the others' malice, were removed, the Lord gave him in the progress of time the power to accomplish his vow of building the aforesaid tower, achieving his heart's desire.

And it came about as though it were told him from heaven: "Well done, good and faithful servant. Since in a few things you were faithful, in many...", etc.

After the death of Abbot Hugo, when a year and months had passed, the lord king sent a letter, commanding that our prior and twelve of the monastery, in whose voice the community's opinion should agree, should appear before him on the assigned day to choose an abbot.

On the day after receiving the letter we met in chapter about how we should manage such a matter.

First, the lord king's letter was read in the monastery; after that we prayed and bound the prior in peril of his soul to name the twelve to go with him, according to his conscience, choosing men whose life and character would ensure that they would not deviate from virtue.

And he, assenting to the request, with the holy spirit speaking, named six from one part of the choir and six from the other and satisfied us with no objection.

But one man said: "What will happen if these thirteen men cannot agree on the selection of an abbot when they are before the king?"

Someone replied that: "This will be an eternal disgrace for us and for our church."

Therefore, more men wanted the election to be held at home before the others left, hoping that this foresight would prevent a disagreement before the king; but it seemed foolish and inappropriate to us to act without the king's assent, since we hadn't yet been able to obtain permission from the lord king to hold a free election.

The under-sacristan Samson, speaking in the Holy Spirit, said: "Let it happen in a middle way, so that from this the peril is avoided.

Let four pious monks and two of the elder monks of the monastery, all of good opinion, get together and, looking upon the relics and touching the Gospels, choose three men of the monastery for this, men qualified according to the rule of Saint Benedict, and let them render their names in writing and seal them up and let the sealed paper be entrusted to us to take to court; and when we have come before the king and he agrees with us about holding a free election, then at last let the seal be broken and thus we will be resolved which three should be named before the king.

And let it be resolved among us, that if the lord king isn't willing to allow us one of our own, then the seal will be carried back unbroken and handed over to the six men who are sworn, in peril of their souls, so that the secret is forever concealed."

We all acquiesced to this advice and four pious monks and another two elders were named.

When this was done we went out, singing "Listen to my words" and the aforesaid six remained, with the Rule of Saint Benedict before their hands, and fulfilled the business as it had been set down.

While these six were discussing this, we were thinking various things about the different choices, with all of us still holding as certain that Samson would be one of the three, considering his labors, and the mortal danger on his journey to Rome for the good of our church and how he'd been straitened and shackled and confined by Abbot Hugo, because he spoke for the advantage of the community. And thus he could not be bent to flattery, although he could be forced into silence.

Now after a delay the monastery was recalled to the chapter house.

And the elders said that they had done what they had been instructed.

AJC On the same day that the thirteen moved out while we were sitting in the cloisters, William of Hastings, one of the brothers, said: "I know that we'll have one of our own for our abbot," and on being asked how he could know this replied that he had seen in a dream a prophet clothed in white standing before the monastery gates and he had asked in the name of the Lord whether we would have an abbot from among us.
