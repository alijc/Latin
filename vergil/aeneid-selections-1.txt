I sing of arms and a man, who, by fate a fugitive,
first came from the shores of Troy to Italy and Lavinia's coast.
Hard was he tossed about on land and sea by heaven's violence,
on account of fierce Juno's wrath, which never forgets.
And too, he suffered much in war, until he might build a city
and carry his gods to Latium; from whence comes the Latin line
and the fathers of Alba and the walls of towering Rome.
Muse, remind me of the reasons, why her divinity was offended
or why the grieving queen of the gods compelled this man of notable goodness
to pass through so many calamities, to undertake so many labors.
Can the celestial souls really hold wrath so great?

There was an ancient city, Carthage (a colony of Tyre),
facing Italy, but distant from the Tiber's mouth,
rich in wealth and most savage in their zeal for war.
Juno thought better of it alone than all other lands,
with even her cherished Samos less esteemed.
Here were her arms, here her chariot;
this kingdom would be a goddess for the people, if the Fates allowed,
at present then she held and fostered it.
But she had once heard that a race that had been sprung from Trojan blood
would topple Tyre's citadels.

...and from there a people and a king, haughty, proud and warlike,
would come to conquer Libya; thus they turned Fates' wheel.
Saturn's daughter was dreading this and mindful of the ancient war
that she first waged at Troy for her beloved Greeks 
(and, truly, not yet had the causes of her wrath and cruel resentment
left her soul; there remains stored deep in her mind 
the judgement of Paris and the insult to her scorned beauty
and the hated race and kidnapped Ganymede's honor).
Thus inflamed the more, she long kept the Trojans,
the survivors of the Greeks and of cruel Achilles,
tossed about across the sea, away from Latium,
and over many years they wandered, driven by the fates the whole sea round.
So great was the burden to found the Roman nation.


The Trojans leave Sicily and Juno begins to consider how to
prevent them from reaching Italy:

Vix e conspectu Siculae telluris in altum

cum Iuno aeternum seruans sub pectore uulnus
haec secum: 'mene incepto desistere uictam
nec posse Italia Teucrorum auertere regem!

Barely out of sight of Sicily the happy men paid out the sails
upon the deep and cut the salty foam with their bronze prows,
when Juno, guarding the everlasting wound within her breast,
said to herself: Am I really defeated with what I have undertaken
and cannot turn the king of the Trojans away from Italy!

But sure, I am forbidden by the Fates.
Didn't Minerva burn the Argives' fleet and drown them in the sea,
due to the injury and rage of a single man, Ajax, Oileus' son?
She herself hurled Jove's swift lightning from the clouds,
then scattered the ships and overturned the sea with the wind.
She seized that man, breathing flames from his transfixed breast,
in a whirlwind and thrust him onto a sharp rock.
But I, the queen of the gods, who walk as
both sister and spouse of Jove,
for so many years have waged war with a single nation.
And will any man hereafter honor Juno's divinity,
or place honors on her altars as a suppliant?

The goddess, mulling so much over in her heart, comes to Aeolia,
the land of storms, pregnant with the raging south winds.
Here, in a vast cave, King Aeolius overwhelms the struggling winds
and boisterous storms by his command, and holds them back with chains and bars.
They rage around the gate,
showing their resentment with a mountainous loud growl;
Aeolius sits in his lofty citadel holding his scepter,
and tames their spirits and tempers their rage;

Aeneas' weary followers hurry to reach the nearest coast,
and turn to Libya's shores.
There's a spot in a vast secluded stretch:
an island makes a harbor by throwing out its flanks.
On these each ocean wave is broken
and splits into the island's inset coves.
From here and there vast cliffs and twin rocks are forced
into the sky, and beneath their summit
far and wide the protected sea is still;
then the background above the trembling woods,
and the murky forest threatens with a dreadful shadow.
Under the facing brow is a cave, made up of hanging rocks;
Within lie sweet waters and benches of living rock,
the home of the Nymphs.  Here no chains hold ships,
no anchor detains with its barbed teeth.
Aeneas puts in here, with his collected ships,
seven out of the total number,
and with great love for the earth
the disembarking Trojans gain the hoped-for beach
and set their frames, rotten with salt, on the shore.
And Achates struck a spark with his flint first
and took the fire up in leaves and put dry fuel
around and kindled a flame in the kindling.
